3D Orthogonal Die Stacking Technology for DRAM-on-GPU Integration 
Using Contactless Die-to-Die Interface 
Yuki Mitarai*1, Masaya Kawano*1, Hung-Chih Huang*1, Takafumi Fukushima*2, 
Tadahiro Kuroda*1, 3, Mototsugu Hamada*1, Atsutake Kosuge*1, 3 
*1: University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan, *2: Tohoku University, Sendai, Japan, *3: RIKEN, Wako, Japan 
 
Abstract 
Massive orthogonal stacking assembly of IC (MOSAIC) 
technology was demonstrated to overcome thermal limitations 
in 3D memory-on-GPU configurations. By stacking memory 
dies orthogonally to the GPU, heat is dissipated effectively, 
enabling twice the memory capacity. A contactless die-to-die 
interface, achieving up to 4 Gbps/ch in a prototype, mitigates 
assembly challenges, enabling TSV-free 3D integration. 
Keywords: Advanced packaging, 3D integration, contactless 
interface. 
Introduction 
The "memory wall" imposes fundamental limits on system 
performance in the AI era, with memory power, bandwidth, 
and capacity as primary bottlenecks. Specifically, for LLM 
inference, now a critical social infrastructure, capacity is 
essential for efficient computation without frequent transfers 
of intermediate values among GPU devices [1]. From a 
hardware perspective, advanced systems typically adopt 2.5D-
packaged HBM today, while 3D HBM-on-GPU integration is 
being actively explored to push performance beyond physical 
constraints and enable further scaling (Fig. 1(a)) [2]. Although 
this integration offers high bandwidth and energy efficiency, 
capacity scaling remains a significant issue. As DRAM density 
scaling slows, increasing the number of stacked dies is highly 
demanded; however, this is strictly limited by thermal issues. 
Although emerging technologies, such as hybrid bonding and 
new materials, are being studied to address this issue [3], they 
have not yet led to a significant increase in the number of 
stacked dies, suggesting a structural limit. 
Proposed method: MOSAIC 
This work presents the world’s first demonstration of a 
massive orthogonal stacking assembly of IC (MOSAIC) 
technology, where memory dies are stacked orthogonally to the 
base die (Fig. 1(b, c)) [4, 5]. Since GPU heat dissipates 
primarily in the vertical direction [2], the vertical alignment of 
high-thermal-conductivity Si substrates effectively suppresses 
peak temperatures, independent of the number of stacked dies. 
We constructed detailed thermal models for both HBM and 
MOSAIC to compare their thermal performances. The HBM 
configuration is based on Ref. [2] and uses the same values, 
except for the use of uniform planar power sources and a 1/4 
symmetric model. The GPU power is set to 300 W, which is 
comparable to AI-oriented GPUs that deliver over 1 PFLOPS 
of BF16 Tensor-core performance [6]. To quantify capacity 
scaling limits, we swept the number of stacked dies from 12 to 
24 under an iso-bandwidth (power) constraint. The MOSAIC 
configuration (Fig. 2) integrates 98 memory dies stacked on the 
GPU. The cube height is set to 5.5 mm, where each memory 
die is half the area of an HBM die.  Power consumption is 
estimated assuming 50 channels per die operating at 8 Gbps/ch, 
with an energy cost of 4 pJ/b per transferred bit for data access 
[7], of which 0.5 pJ/b is attributed to I/O energy [8]. The results 
are summarized in Fig. 3. While a 12 Hi HBM reached a peak 
temperature of 80 °C, doubling the capacity with a 24 Hi HBM 
increased the peak to 97 °C, crossing the thermal threshold of 
95 °C [9]. By comparison, MOSAIC accommodates the same 
twofold capacity while suppressing the peak temperature to 
81.3 ºC, showing significantly improved heat dissipation. 
Implementation of a MOSAIC cube 
In our proposed cube, each memory die receives power 
through sidewall electrodes, while data transmission is 
performed using a contactless interface. Fig. 4 illustrates its 
assembly flow. Compared with HBM, the proposed cube offers 
several advantages in its assembly: (1) elimination of TSVs; 
(2) removal of physical electrical contacts, reducing the risk of 
ESD-induced damage; and (3) direct communication between 
each memory die and GPU, enabling system-level redundancy 
and offering the potential for W2W integration. 
The key challenge is the die-to-die interface between the 
GPU and the memory cube. Conventional contact -based 
interfaces are unreliable due to surface irregularity and 
misalignment. This work utilizes a right -angle proximity 
inductive coupling interface, enabling stable communication 
over varying distances, including lateral offsets and 
communication distance variations (Fig. 5). 
Right-Angle Inductive Coupling Interface  
This work employs an oblong coil design for the interface, 
which maintains a sufficient coupling coefficient and reduces 
crosstalk from adjacent coil pairs while reducing the footprint 
compared to square coils [10]. The long and short sides are set 
to 240 µm and 80 µm, respectively, enabling the realization of 
50 channels with a pitch of 400 µm on a 22 mm-wide die as 
assumed in the thermal simulation. 
A limitation of oblong coils is their reduced tolerance to 
misalignment, particularly along the short side of the base die 
coil (Y-axis). In this direction, the small gap between the long 
edges carrying antiparallel currents causes large cancellation 
of signal coupling when the coil is displaced. Using a triple 
overlaid coils design at the base die constructed in the same 
two metal layers as the single coil, misalignment tolerance was 
enhanced by 41% within the same footprint (Fig. 6). 
Experimental Results and Discussion 
To evaluate the MOSAIC implementation and its interface 
performance, a test cube was fabricated with 0.18 μm MPW 
shuttle chips (Fig. 7). The cube consists of eight stacked 2.4 
mm × 2.4 mm daughter dies placed on a 2.5 mm × 5 mm base 
die. Power is supplied through sidewall metal contacts via 
solder and an FPC board. Assembly feasibility and coil 
placement accuracy were verified using X -ray computed 
tomography. The daughter-die coil is positioned over the 
corresponding base-die coil within the acceptable alignment at 
a communication distance of 17 µm (Fig. 8).   
The interface test was performed using a loopback path 
between the base die and the active daughter die (Fig. 9). A 4 
Gbps PRBS27-1 signal was used to evaluate two 
communication paths, and in both cases, the signal was 
successfully recovered by activating the optimal coil in the 
triple-stacked structure (Fig. 10), achieving an average energy 
efficiency of 6.9 pJ/b. This result was obtained using a 0.18 µm 
process, and further reduction in energy consumption and 
higher data rates (e.g., 8 Gb/s at 0.5 pJ/bit) are expected with 
more advanced process technologies [8, 11].

MOSAIC doubles the memory capacity at equivalent 
bandwidth and peak temperature compared to the HBM-on-
GPU baseline [2] and enables a larger number of stacked dies 
under a higher GPU operating power environment than that 
reported in [3]. In addition, further capacity scaling is expected 
by thinning the silicon substrate. Thinning the 300 µm die to 
100 µm enables a three-fold capacity increase with a negligible 
0.04 ºC rise in peak temperature without significant crosstalk
